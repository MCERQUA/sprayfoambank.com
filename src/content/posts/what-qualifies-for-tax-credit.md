The federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit gets mentioned constantly in insulation sales conversations, often with more confidence about specifics than is warranted. Here's a careful, appropriately hedged look at what actually tends to qualify. This is not tax advice — confirm current-year specifics with a tax professional or IRS.gov.

## Materials, Generally

Insulation materials that meet relevant energy-efficiency criteria — including spray foam products meeting applicable standards — have generally been eligible in recent years. The exact certification requirements a product must meet, and whether installation labor is included in the eligible cost, are the kind of specifics that have varied and are worth confirming for your actual filing year rather than assuming based on general knowledge.

## It's Your Primary Residence, Generally

These credits have generally applied to a taxpayer's primary residence, with different or no eligibility for rental or investment properties — another detail worth confirming rather than assuming applies to your specific property situation.

## Annual Caps Exist

The credit has generally been subject to an annual dollar cap across eligible improvement categories, meaning a very large project might not have its full cost covered by the credit percentage in a single tax year. This is a real, structurally important detail — and exactly the kind of specific number that changes with legislation, so we're not printing a figure here as if it's guaranteed current.

## Why "My Contractor Said It Qualifies" Isn't Enough

A contractor recommending a product they say qualifies is not the same as tax-filing certainty. The responsible move is asking your contractor for the specific manufacturer certification documentation, then confirming eligibility with a tax professional or directly via IRS Form 5695 instructions (the form typically used to claim these credits) before you file.

## The Practical Takeaway

Ask your installer for an itemized invoice and any manufacturer certification statement before the job is done, not after you're filing taxes months later. Keep that documentation with your other tax records. When you're ready to file, a tax professional (or careful reading of current IRS Form 5695 instructions) is the reliable source for exact percentages and caps — not a sales conversation.
